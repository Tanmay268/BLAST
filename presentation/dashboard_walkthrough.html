How to use this

The six sections below match the six tabs in the dashboard's sidebar, in the same order. Read a section right before you click that tab. A glossary and a "likely questions" section sit at the end for quick reference during Q&A.

00

The one-sentence idea

When several things break in a microservice system at once, most tools rank them by how technically severe each one looks on its own — error rate, latency spike, CPU usage. BLAST's claim is that this is the wrong unit of analysis, because broken things often overlap in what they affect. If incident A and incident B both knock out the same business feature, fixing A already recovers most of the value B was going to cost you — B's true priority just dropped, even though nothing about B itself changed.

BLAST treats "which incident first?" as picking a small set of incidents to cover the most business-critical capabilities, not scoring incidents one at a time. That's a classic submodular set-selection problem — the same mathematical family behind influence-maximization and sensor placement — which is why a simple greedy algorithm comes with a provable quality guarantee, not just an intuition.

Where BLAST starts and stops: it never detects faults, localizes root causes, or reads logs. It assumes some upstream system has already told it "these N services are broken right now" — BLAST's only job is deciding the order to fix them in.

What "mixed" actually means

BLAST is compared against B9, a version of itself with the set-selection logic switched off — it just scores every incident independently and sorts. This is the single most important comparison in the project, because it isolates exactly one thing: does thinking about overlap between incidents help?

  • On NDCG@5 (how close BLAST's ordering is to the true best ordering) — yes, clearly. The gap is real by two separate tests: a p-value near zero, and an effect size (Cliff's δ = 0.277) past the "this isn't just noise" threshold.
  • On Cumulative Business Loss — the more practical metric, "how much value did you lose while waiting for fixes" — the improvement is technically detectable (helped by having 90 test scenarios, which makes even tiny effects statistically visible) but the effect size is almost zero. In plain terms: BLAST orders things more accurately, but at these scenario sizes it barely changes how much damage accumulates before everything gets fixed.

Why report it this way instead of picking the flattering number: a project log that only reports p-values would call this an unqualified win. Applying the same effect-size bar used everywhere else in the project to this result too is what makes the "win" honest — it also happens to be more interesting: it says where the method's real strength is (ordering accuracy) and where it isn't proven yet (loss reduction).

The rest of the page

A bar chart compares all eight methods on NDCG@5 and business loss side by side; a footprint statistic reports how many of the capabilities a typical incident actually touches (never all of them, once attribution was fixed); and an expander lists exactly what the project does not claim — no real revenue was measured, scenarios are synthesized from single faults, and so on.

02

Ranked Incident Queue

sidebar → Ranked Incident Queue

The only screen where you build something yourself. Pick two or more incident types from a dropdown — say, a CPU fault on the currency service and a network-loss fault on the recommendation service — and the page computes, live in your browser, the order BLAST would fix them in, next to the order the "no set-selection" baseline would use.

What's actually running

Each incident type carries a probability of impairing each of the 9 business capabilities, learned from its three real repetitions in the dataset. BLAST repeatedly asks: "of the incidents I haven't picked yet, which one adds the most expected coverage of still-unprotected capabilities?" — and picks that one first. Each card shows exactly what it newly covers, which is the whole submodular idea made visible: later picks in the queue add less because earlier picks already covered some of the same ground.

The bottom of the page runs the composed scenario against the same measured ground truth the real evaluation uses, and reports the actual simulated business loss under each ordering — a smaller, live version of the Gate 4 experiment.

Presentation tip: pick a scenario where a service's incidents share a lot of overlap (e.g. two currency-service faults) alongside one that doesn't (an unrelated email-service fault). BLAST will visibly promote the non-overlapping one earlier than the independent-scoring baseline does — that's the concept in one click.

03

Business Dependency Graph

sidebar → Business Dependency Graph

A node-and-edge picture of which services call which, reconstructed directly from real request traces (not hand-drawn) — 7 services, 9 call edges. Node size and color reflect PageRank, a measure of structural centrality: checkoutservice and frontendservice sit at the center because most user journeys pass through them.

The table underneath is the more important part

For every edge, the table reports how often a fault in the source service was rigorously shown to also degrade the target service — real statistical testing (a significance test and an effect-size floor), not a guess. The honest number is 0% on every tested edge. Faults in this dataset stay contained to the service they were injected into; they don't meaningfully spread along the call graph.

Why this doesn't sink the project: it changes which half of the idea is doing the work. BLAST has two conceptual halves — "faults propagate across the graph" and "concurrent incidents overlap in what they cover." The propagation half turned out to be weak on this benchmark; the overlap half (Section 01/02) is where the real signal is. Saying this plainly is more credible than quietly dropping the graph view.

04

Capability Path Viewer

sidebar → Capability Path Viewer

Pick a service and a fault type, and see exactly which business capability got hit and why. Each row reads left to right: service → the specific user journey that got measurably slower or failed → the business capability that journey delivers. For example, a delay fault on checkoutservice degrades the Place Order journey, which realizes the Order Placement capability — a direct, traceable chain, not a guess.

The bug this screen exists to demonstrate the fix for

An earlier version attributed impact at the service level: "was checkoutservice generally slower?" That sounds reasonable, but checkoutservice's own outbound calls (to currency, payment, email…) got misattributed to checkoutservice itself, so almost any fault appeared to hit nearly every business capability at once — useless for prioritization, since everything looked equally bad. Re-attributing impact at the level of the actual user journey that got worse fixed this: now different incidents visibly hit different capabilities, which is what makes set-selection meaningful in the first place.

Reading the result column

Every comparison is classified using two checks at once: is the difference statistically real (a paired significance test, corrected for testing many comparisons at once), and is it large enough to matter (an effect-size floor). Only rows clearing both read BLAST wins (real). Two honest results worth pointing out live side by side here: the plain severity and ITIL baselines actually out-rank BLAST on pure ranking accuracy — traced to the fact that, in this dataset, how badly a fault hits tracks its business cost almost as well as what it hits does, which weakens the advantage of business-aware weighting — while the pure-topology baselines (B4/B5/B6/B11/B12) all lose clearly to BLAST, since graph centrality alone doesn't predict business impact nearly as well as severity does on this benchmark.

06

Heterogeneity Sweep

sidebar → Heterogeneity Sweep

A follow-up experiment, not on the real dataset at all — a simulation. The natural explanation for Section 01's mixed business-loss result was "maybe the real incidents just don't overlap enough for set-selection to pay off." This screen tests that directly: it generates synthetic incident types with a dial for exactly how much their business-capability footprints overlap, from identical (like the real dataset) to completely disjoint, and reruns the BLAST-vs-independent comparison at every setting.

The honest result: the hypothesis didn't hold. The independent baseline beat BLAST on business loss at every overlap level tested, including zero overlap — not what was predicted. A follow-up check ruled out the obvious bug (a mismatch in how the two methods combine overlapping incidents); it wasn't that. The working explanation now is that minimizing business loss under a fixed one-at-a-time repair schedule is closer to a scheduling problem — where sorting by value is already a strong strategy — than to the coverage-maximizing problem BLAST's greedy algorithm is mathematically proven to do well on. Those turned out to not be the same problem.

This is presented as a real, useful negative result: it narrows down why the business-loss number didn't move, rather than leaving it unexplained.

07

Glossary

NDCG@5
How close a ranking's top 5 are to the true best top 5, on a 0–1 scale. 1.0 is a perfect match.
Cumulative Business Loss
Simulates fixing incidents one at a time in a given order and adds up the business value still broken at each step. Lower is better.
Cliff's delta
An effect-size measure: how often one method's results actually beat the other's, not just whether the average differs. Below ≈0.15 is treated as noise-sized in this project.
p-value / Holm correction
The odds a difference this large would appear by chance. Holm correction tightens the bar when many comparisons are run at once, so a few get lucky by chance.
Submodular
A "diminishing returns" property: adding an incident to a small set helps at least as much as adding it to a bigger set that already covers more ground. It's what gives greedy selection its quality guarantee.
Journey
One real end-to-end user request through the system, reconstructed from a distributed trace — e.g. one "place an order" attempt.
Capability
A business-facing feature a journey delivers, e.g. Order Placement or Product Browsing — the unit BLAST actually optimizes for, not raw technical health.
RE2-OB
The benchmark dataset: 90 real fault-injection experiments on Online Boutique, a demo e-commerce microservice app — 5 services × 6 fault types × 3 repeats.
08

If a faculty member asks…

"So does it actually work, yes or no?"

It ranks incidents more accurately than scoring them independently — that part is real, statistically and practically. It hasn't yet been shown to reduce simulated business loss by a meaningful margin on this specific benchmark, and the follow-up experiment (Section 06) narrows down why, rather than leaving that unexplained.

"Why trust a synthetic value model instead of real revenue?"

No public incident dataset has real revenue attached. The business weights are declared openly as an assumption (never claimed as measured), and the project checks how much the results depend on that assumption rather than hiding it.

"Isn't 90 test scenarios small?"

It's enough to detect real differences reliably — which is exactly how the "statistically significant but practically negligible" business-loss result was caught rather than overstated: a small effect still showed up as "significant" at this sample size, which is precisely why the effect-size check matters alongside the p-value.

"What would you do next?"

Test on a second system with a different call topology (the current graph-propagation result may be specific to this app), and separately, work out whether the ranking algorithm can be changed to directly target business-loss reduction rather than capability coverage — since Section 06 suggests those are related but not identical objectives.
